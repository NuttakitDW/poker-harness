# Poker and finance analogies

- Stable ID: `21-poker-and-finance-analogies`
- Scope: Historical analogy, not investment guidance or a direct transfer of poker formulas.
- Thai counterpart: [อุปมาโป๊กเกอร์กับการเงิน](../../TH/topics/21-poker-and-finance-analogies.md)

## Core idea

Poker and financial decisions both involve uncertain outcomes, changing information, risk limits and incentives. The useful comparison is process: estimate possible outcomes, their likelihood, downside exposure and the cost of new information. A profitable expected value can still be unacceptable if a loss threatens solvency. The analogy breaks when market prices, counterparties, liquidity, regulations and feedback differ from a poker hand. Read the source as a historical perspective on risk and incentives, then test each claimed transfer separately.

## Where the analogy helps and fails

A poker player and a trader can both keep a decision journal, compare expected gain with downside, and separate a good decision from a lucky result. Both need enough reserve to survive a sequence of adverse outcomes. But a poker hand has a relatively explicit ruleset and payout, while financial markets have changing counterparties, prices and liquidity. Poker pot-odds formulas do not automatically price an asset.

Authored numerical check: A bet winning $12 with 60% probability and losing $10 otherwise has EV .60×12 − .40×10 = +$3.20. If the loss amount were $10,000 instead, the same 60% win rate would not imply a positive EV. Always state both size and probability of each outcome. This is a reasoning example, not a recommendation to place the wager.

## Worked example (authored; not quoted from source)

Authored example: A wager winning $600 with 95% probability and losing $10,000 with 5% probability has EV +$70 (.95×600 − .05×10,000), yet the downside may be unacceptable if $10,000 is the entire reserve.

## Practice and boundary checks

Risk of ruin illustrates why averaging alone is incomplete. A strategy can have positive expected gain yet create an unacceptable probability of exhausting the funds needed to continue. In poker, one can sometimes reduce stakes and continue; in a financial market, positions may be difficult to liquidate at the expected price. These differences matter more than the superficial fact that both domains involve bets. Use the book to ask better questions about incentives, information and downside, then seek domain-specific evidence before applying any concrete rule.

## Common errors

Assuming a poker edge predicts a market edge; ignoring the risk of ruin.
